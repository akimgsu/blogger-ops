Week 9: New Media Policies and Global Broadcasting Trends

Entering the 1980s, Cable TV emerged as a powerful "New Media," delivering rich content over dedicated telecom infrastructure rather than open airwaves. This week, we examine how the cable industry shattered its regulatory shackles in the US, while also exploring the dynamic history of media control and liberalization across Europe.

1. The Cable Communications Policy Act of 1984 and the NCTA

In its infancy, Cable TV struggled against a dense web of regulations imposed by local municipalities and lobbied for by traditional over-the-air broadcasters. The turning point that ignited the industry's explosive growth was the passage of the Cable Communications Policy Act of 1984.

The primary interest group that spearheaded the negotiations and lobbied heavily for this deregulatory act was the NCTA (National Cable Television Association). Their efforts successfully freed cable operators from stringent local rate regulations, transforming cable into a highly profitable national industry.

2. Turner Broadcasting and the Must-Carry Constitutional Battle

As cable television began to overpower traditional terrestrial broadcasting, the government imposed Must-Carry regulations. These laws forced cable operators to transmit local broadcast channels on their systems, ostensibly to protect local news and public information.

Ted Turner’s media empire, Turner Broadcasting, furiously pushed back, challenging the regulations in two monumental Supreme Court cases (1994 and 1997). The core legal argument Turner used to challenge Must-Carry was a Violation of the First Amendment Right to Speech.

Turner argued that forcing a private cable platform to broadcast channels it did not want to carry was an infringement on their editorial control and freedom of expression. This case remains a foundational precedent regarding the First Amendment rights of media platforms.

3. European Policies: Pirate Broadcasters, Borderless TV, and BT Privatization

Meanwhile, Europe was experiencing its own unique clashes over media regulation.

Pirate Broadcasters

In the 1960s, state-run European broadcasters strictly controlled the airwaves, heavily restricting rock and pop music. In rebellion, "Pirate Broadcasters" set up transmitters on ships and platforms anchored in international waters—specifically, Offshore marine broadcasting stations. From there, beyond the reach of national jurisdiction, they blasted popular music to eager audiences across the continent.

Television Without Frontiers Directive (1989)

Passed in 1989, this directive was the cornerstone of the European Union’s audiovisual policy, designed to remove barriers to broadcasting across European member states. However, it is vital to note that it did not accomplish "Free trade in TV programs with all global markets." It was heavily protectionist regarding non-European (especially American) content.

Privatization of British Telecom

The Margaret Thatcher administration's push to privatize the state-owned monopoly British Telecom (BT) was highly controversial. The assertion that the privatization of British Telecom enjoyed broad support among the British public is unequivocally False.

Chapter Summary

  • 1984 Cable Act: Pushed through by the NCTA, this act deregulated the cable industry, sparking massive growth.
  • First Amendment Fight: Turner Broadcasting sued against Must-Carry regulations, arguing they violated their constitutional right to free speech.
  • Offshore Radio: 1960s pirate radio bypassed European state media monopolies by broadcasting from offshore marine stations.
  • European Policy Facts: The 1989 EU Television directive did not open free trade globally, and BT's privatization was highly unpopular with the British public.
