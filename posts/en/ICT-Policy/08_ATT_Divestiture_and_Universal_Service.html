Week 8: The Great Telecom Restructuring - AT&T Divestiture and Universal Service

This week, we cover one of the most dramatic corporate breakups in history: the divestiture of the American telecommunications monopoly, AT&T (The Bell System). It was a period of intense philosophical conflict over whether telecom infrastructure was merely a corporate profit engine or a fundamental right for all citizens.

1. Universal Service and Cross-Subsidies

For decades, AT&T fiercely defended its monopoly status by championing the concept of Universal Service—the principle that every citizen, regardless of whether they lived in a dense city or a remote farm, should have access to the telephone network at an affordable price.

To achieve this, AT&T utilized a system of Cross-subsidies. They took the massive profits generated from their highly lucrative long-distance and corporate services and used that money to subsidize the loss-making local networks in low-population rural areas. AT&T argued that introducing competition would destroy these cross-subsidies, leaving rural Americans disconnected.

2. The AT&T Divestiture: The 1982 Consent Decree

However, technological advancements and demands from competitors (like MCI) to enter the long-distance market pushed the monopoly to its breaking point. This culminated in the largest antitrust action in US history.

A critical historical fact is that the breakup of AT&T was not mandated by an act of Congress. Legally, the divestiture was the product of a Settlement between AT&T and the Justice Department (DOJ), codified in the 1982 Consent Decree.

The Flawed Defense (Exam Note)

While AT&T made many arguments to defend itself against divestiture, one argument they did not prioritize was asserting that "Divestiture would destroy a US competitive advantage in international trade in telecommunication services." At the time, this was fundamentally a domestic antitrust battle, not a global trade issue.

The Result: 7 RBOCs

When the breakup took effect in 1984, the monolith was shattered. AT&T retained its long-distance operations, while local telephone service was spun off into exactly 7 Regional Bell Operating Companies (RBOCs), colloquially known as the "Baby Bells." This fragmentation triggered fierce competition and paved the physical pathway for the internet revolution.

3. The Public Broadcasting Act of 1967 and the CPB

Alongside telecom restructuring, the broadcast media landscape also faced a push for public interest reforms. By the 1960s, American television was thoroughly dominated by highly commercial networks focused on ratings-driven entertainment.

Prompted by reports from the Carnegie and Ford Foundations, Congress passed the Public Broadcasting Act of 1967. The explicit intention was to supplement commercial mass media with high-quality educational and cultural programming. This legislation successfully established the Corporation for Public Broadcasting (CPB), which to this day serves as the vital funding arm for PBS and NPR.

Chapter Summary

  • Universal Service & Cross-subsidies: AT&T justified its monopoly by using highly profitable long-distance revenues to subsidize unprofitable rural local networks.
  • A Legal Settlement: The monumental breakup of AT&T was a legal settlement between the company and the Justice Department, not a congressional law.
  • 7 Baby Bells: The divestiture shattered the local telecom market into 7 Regional Bell Operating Companies (RBOCs).
  • Public Broadcasting (1967): To counter the hyper-commercialization of media, the CPB was established to fund and support educational programming.
