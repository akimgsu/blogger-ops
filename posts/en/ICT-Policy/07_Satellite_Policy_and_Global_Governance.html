Week 7: Satellite Communications Policy and Global Governance

As we enter Part 3 (Telecom Restructuring, Satellite, Cable, and the Birth of the Internet), we look up at the dawn of the communications satellite era. The Cold War space race dramatically accelerated telecom infrastructure, leading to massive domestic and international clashes over who would own and control these expensive orbital assets.

1. The Debate Over Satellite Development Models

Before launching the first commercial communications satellites, the US political landscape fractured into a three-way debate regarding the business model for this massive national infrastructure.

  1. Progressives in the Democratic Party:
    Position: Argued that space is a public asset and therefore satellite networks should be a government-owned corporation.
  2. Eisenhower Administration:
    Position: Advocated for developing satellites with the incumbents (the existing telecom monopolies like AT&T) to leverage private capital and existing terrestrial infrastructure.
  3. Kennedy-Johnson Administration:
    Position: Worried about expanding existing telecom monopolies into space but wanting to avoid government inefficiency, they pushed to develop satellites with a new corporation (which became COMSAT). This middle-ground model ultimately prevailed.

2. Historic Satellite Projects

During the early space race, various organizations scrambled to launch satellites. Matching these historic projects with their developers is crucial for understanding the era.

  • Sputnik → Soviet Union: Launched in 1957, humanity's first artificial satellite, sparking the "Sputnik Shock" and igniting the space race.
  • Echo → NASA / Bell Labs: An early passive balloon satellite reflecting microwave signals.
  • RELAY → NASA / RCA: An active repeater satellite developed in partnership with RCA to counterbalance AT&T's dominance.
  • TELSTAR → NASA / AT&T: The historic privately sponsored satellite that successfully relayed the first transatlantic live television signals.

3. The Voting Structure of INTELSAT's Board of Governors

Following the creation of COMSAT, the US led the formation of INTELSAT (International Telecommunications Satellite Organization) to unite the globe under a single satellite network.

The most important governance feature of INTELSAT was its method of allocating votes on the Board of Governors. Unlike the UN General Assembly, INTELSAT did not adopt an egalitarian "one country, one vote" system.

Instead, they adopted a strictly capitalistic and pragmatic approach: voting power was assigned proportional to financial contributions. Consequently, the nations (primarily the US) that invested the most capital into building the satellite network wielded the most voting power and control, maximizing investment incentives.

Chapter Summary

  • The 3-Way Policy Debate: Progressives wanted government-owned, Eisenhower wanted incumbents, and Kennedy-Johnson won by establishing a completely new corporation (COMSAT).
  • Historic Matches: Sputnik (USSR), Echo (NASA/Bell), RELAY (NASA/RCA), and TELSTAR (NASA/AT&T).
  • INTELSAT Governance: Votes were distributed proportionally based on financial contributions, rejecting the standard "one nation, one vote" international paradigm.
