Week 4: The Telegraph Revolution and the Birth of International Regulation

As we move into Part 2 (The Historical Development of Telecommunication Technology and Early Regulatory Systems), we turn back the clock to the 19th century—the era that laid the foundation for modern telecommunications. This week, we analyze the invention of the electrical telegraph and its decisive impact on global financial markets, as well as the dawn of international telecommunications treaties.

1. The Telegraph and the Revolution of Financial Markets

After Samuel Morse transmitted the famous first message, "What hath God wrought," in 1844, telegraph technology began to wire the world together. The sector where this technology generated the highest profits and most revolutionary changes was, undoubtedly, the Financial Markets.

Information on stock and commodity prices, which previously took days to travel, began to be transmitted near-instantly via the telegraph. This spawned the explosive growth of the Ticker Tape system, which quickly became the most lucrative business model on the early telecom networks. It is the very first historical instance of telecommunications infrastructure redefining the speed of capital markets.

2. The Dawn of Multilateral Treaties: The West European Telegraph Union

As telecommunication networks expanded across borders, a severe problem arose: Every time a telegram crossed a border, technical standards, tariff calculation methods, and decoding rules varied wildly from nation to nation.

To resolve this friction, the West European Telegraph Union was formed in 1855. It stands as one of the earliest multilateral international treaties designed to standardize cross-border telecommunications operations, unifying technical specifications and tariff regimes. The creation of this union laid the ideological and institutional groundwork for the modern International Telecommunication Union (ITU).

3. Submarine Cables and Global Geopolitics

Submarine cables stretching across the ocean floor were not only the pinnacle of engineering at the time, but also critical weapons in imperial geopolitics.

Private Sector Financing

While it is easy to assume that massive telecommunication infrastructure projects were government-led, the first transatlantic telegraph cables were primarily financed by risk-taking investors from the Private Sector, most notably Cyrus West Field, rather than state budgets.

The British Empire's All-Red Line

Great Britain, which ruled the seas, realized that the security of communications was directly tied to national security. To prevent foreign wiretapping or sabotage, they engineered a global submarine cable network that only landed on British-controlled soil or colonies. Following the cartographic tradition of coloring British territories red, this highly secure network was famously dubbed the All-Red Line (or network). It is a prime example of the early "weaponization" of telecom infrastructure.

The 1884 Submarine Cable Convention

As deliberate cable sabotage became frequent, the international community established the 1884 Submarine Cable Convention to criminalize peacetime cable destruction. However, this treaty contained a crucial loophole: it explicitly exempted Belligerents (nations at war). By allowing nations to sever an enemy's cables during wartime without violating international law, the treaty officially recognized telecommunications as a vital military asset.

Chapter Summary

  • Telegraph and Finance: The real-time transmission of stock and commodity prices via the Ticker Tape system drove the early commercial viability of the telegraph.
  • First Multilateral Regulation: The 1855 West European Telegraph Union pioneered the standardization of technical specs and tariffs across national borders.
  • Early Submarine Cables: Driven heavily by private sector capital and investments rather than pure government initiatives.
  • Security and Infrastructure: The British Empire built the highly secure 'All-Red network', and the 1884 Convention legally allowed 'belligerents' to cut enemy cables during wartime.
