Week 13: Platform Regulation, Free Speech, and Content Moderation

As the internet has evolved into a massive global public square, the question of how much responsibility Big Tech platforms (Facebook, Google, etc.) bear for the content they host has become a critical global agenda. This week, we analyze the foundational laws that created the modern platform business model, and the fierce political and international efforts to reel them in.

1. CDA Section 230 and the "Good Samaritan" Provision

The single most important legal foundation that allowed Silicon Valley platforms to grow exponentially without drowning in lawsuits is Section 230 of the Communications Decency Act (1996). It established that platforms cannot be held legally liable as the "publisher" of content created by their users.

A crucial component of this law is the "Good Samaritan" provision. In the past, if a platform actively deleted harmful content, courts might deem them a "publisher" exercising editorial control, thereby exposing them to massive legal liability. The Good Samaritan provision fixed this contradiction. By removing the fear of liability, the provision explicitly encouraged platforms to block or screen material that was offensive or harmful, sparking a wave of self-regulation and content moderation.

2. Restricting Immunities: The EARN-IT Act of 2020

Recently, amid outcries over fake news, hate speech, and illegal content, public pressure to reform Section 230's blanket immunity has skyrocketed.

A prominent legislative attempt is the proposed EARN-IT Act of 2020. This bill sought to conditionally restrict Section 230 immunities for platforms that failed to adopt designated "best practices." To garner overwhelming bipartisan support, the primary social justification the bill appealed to was the Prevention of Online Child Sexual Exploitation. It marks a highly symbolic shift toward stricter legal accountability for platforms.

3. International Cooperation on Terrorist Content

Because extremist content circulated by terrorist organizations transcends borders, robust cooperation among Big Tech companies and international organizations is mandatory.

  • GIFCT (Global Internet Forum to Counter Terrorism): A powerful consortium founded by major American tech companies. Crucially, they do not share user IPs or login credentials. Instead, they facilitate a program to share a hash database of terrorist images and video. If a terrorist video is blocked on one platform, its digital "hash" (fingerprint) is shared, allowing other platforms to instantly block it upon upload.
  • OECD (Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development): A prominent international organization actively working on the development of standardized reporting protocols for allegedly terrorist content across jurisdictions.

4. The Digital Divide vs. Net Neutrality: Facebook's "Free Basics"

Efforts by Big Tech to connect developing nations have sometimes collided violently with national sovereignty and Net Neutrality laws.

The most famous example is Facebook's "Free Basics" program. Facebook offered users in developing markets free mobile data access (zero-rating)—but only to a limited subset of websites handpicked by Facebook and its partners.

While Facebook marketed this as a philanthropic effort to bridge the digital divide, local activists condemned it as a severe violation of Net Neutrality designed to trap users inside a Facebook-controlled walled garden. Ultimately, the government of India banned the service outright in 2018, making it an issue of massive national and global importance.

Chapter Summary

  • CDA Section 230: The Good Samaritan provision legally protected and explicitly encouraged platforms to proactively block or screen harmful content.
  • EARN-IT Act (2020): Attempted to roll back Section 230 protections under the powerful justification of Preventing Online Child Sexual Exploitation.
  • International Cooperation: The GIFCT rapidly removes content by sharing a hash database of terrorist media, while the OECD develops standardized reporting protocols.
  • Free Basics Clash: Facebook's attempt to offer free access to a restricted subset of the internet violated Net Neutrality principles and was famously banned in India in 2018.
