Week 14: Platform Antitrust and Mega-Mergers

As Big Tech platforms have grown at an unprecedented rate, they have completely overshadowed traditional legacy media companies. This week, we analyze how governments attempt to reign in digital monopolies through antitrust regulations, and how traditional media giants are fighting for survival through massive mergers and acquisitions (M&A).

1. Internet Platforms vs. Legacy Cable TV Market Caps

The clearest indicator of the tectonic shift in modern economic power is Market Capitalization. Currently, the market cap of major internet platforms (like Google, Meta, Apple, Amazon) is significantly larger than that of traditional cable television companies (like Comcast or Charter).

Legacy telecom operators face the existential threat of becoming mere "dumb pipes," while tech platforms rake in massive advertising and data profits running on top of those pipes. This extreme imbalance in financial firepower is a primary driver behind modern M&A strategies and antitrust scrutiny.

2. The European Commission's Antitrust Case Against Google

The European Union remains the most aggressive regulator of American Big Tech monopolies.

A highly symbolic antitrust action was the European Commission's (EC) first case against Google, initiated in 2010. The EC accused Google of abusing its dominant position in internet search by illegally manipulating its algorithms to push users to its own comparison-shopping service. This landmark case legally challenged the concept of "Search Neutrality," ruling that a platform cannot unfairly advantage its own downstream services over competitors.

3. Analyzing the 1994 Microsoft Consent Decree

The modern era of tech antitrust essentially began in the 1990s with the Department of Justice targeting Microsoft's dominance over PC operating systems, leading to the 1994 Consent Decree.

  • Terms Included in the Decree: Microsoft was required to provide technical information about their OS on a nondiscriminatory basis, eliminate the tying of additional products to the sale of MS-DOS, and remove pricing penalties for PC manufacturers who sold PCs with competing Operating Systems.
  • Exam Note (What was NOT in the decree): The 1994 Consent Decree did not force Microsoft to add a competing browser product by default. That was related to subsequent Browser War litigation later in the decade.

4. The AT&T / Time Warner Vertical Merger (2018)

Desperate to compete with the rising dominance of tech platforms (like Netflix and Amazon), telecom giant AT&T sought to acquire the massive content producer Time Warner. Because AT&T (distribution) and Time Warner (content) operated at different levels of the supply chain, this was a massive Vertical Merger.

The DOJ sued to block the merger, but on June 21, 2018, Judge Richard Leon allowed the merger to proceed without conditions. His assertions included:

  • Noting the arrival of intense new forms of competition from big internet platforms and streaming media.
  • Rejecting the government's vertical leverage argument, which claimed AT&T would withhold content to crush competitors.
  • Citing a lack of precedent for blocking vertical mergers in recent judicial history.

(Exam Note: Judge Leon did not base his decision on asserting that the concentration of the market does not yet meet the legal standard. He fundamentally rejected the government's economic models rather than citing a numerical concentration threshold.)

Chapter Summary

  • Market Cap Shift: The market cap of internet platforms is currently significantly larger than that of cable television companies.
  • Google Antitrust (EC): The EU accused Google of illegally pushing search users toward its own comparison-shopping service.
  • Microsoft Decree (1994): Eliminated MS-DOS tying and pricing penalties, but did not mandate the default inclusion of a competing browser.
  • Judge Leon's Ruling (2018): Allowed the AT&T / Time Warner vertical merger to proceed unconditionally, citing new streaming competition and rejecting vertical leverage theories. (He did not assert market concentration was below legal standards).
