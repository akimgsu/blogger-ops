Week 10: Spectrum Auctions and the Commercialization of the Internet

As the 1990s dawned, the telecommunications paradigm experienced a dramatic shift. The invisible resource known as radio spectrum—previously allocated by the state through bureaucratic means—was introduced to the capitalist mechanics of "Auctions." Simultaneously, the "Internet," which had been confined to academics and researchers, finally exploded into a commercial network for the masses.

1. The 1993 Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act and Spectrum Auctions

Historically, the government allocated spectrum to companies via comparative hearings or lotteries. These methods were notoriously inefficient and often resulted in allegations of favoritism or wasted economic value.

To rectify this, the US Congress passed the Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1993 (OBRA), which formally legalized Spectrum Auctions for the first time. This was a monumental event in applying game theory and auction theory to the real world. By forcing companies to bid capital for frequency bands, the government ensured that the spectrum went to those who valued it most highly (maximizing market efficiency) while simultaneously generating billions of dollars in public revenue.

2. Transition to a Commercial Internet: MERIT, ANS, and NSFNET

The predecessor to today's commercial internet was the NSFNET, an academic network funded by the National Science Foundation. By the early 1990s, the push to open this network to commercial users had begun.

A pivotal player in this transition was a non-profit organization named MERIT. MERIT was a network originally created by universities located in the state of Michigan. Partnering with telecom giants IBM and MCI, MERIT spun off a new entity called ANS (Advanced Network Services). ANS became a pioneer by marketing internet access directly to commercial users who were outside the scope of traditional education and research networks, effectively firing the starting gun for the commercial internet era.

3. IP Address Management: RIRs, IPv4, and IPv6

As the internet commercialized and the number of connected devices skyrocketed, managing the identification system—IP addresses—became critical.

Regional Internet Registries (RIRs)

IP addresses are not handed out to individual users by a single global dictator. Instead, the global address space is managed by continental bodies known as Regional Internet Registries (RIRs). These organizations are responsible for allocating blocks of IP address numbers down to the local Internet Service Providers (ISPs).

Address Space: IPv4 vs. IPv6

  • IPv4: The legacy IP addressing system that uses 32 bits. It provides roughly 4.3 billion addresses, a limit we have practically exhausted due to the proliferation of smartphones and IoT devices.
  • IPv6: The next-generation protocol designed to solve the exhaustion problem, utilizing a massive 128 bits. It provides enough unique addresses to assign one to every grain of sand on Earth multiple times over.

Chapter Summary

  • 1993 OBRA: The landmark legislation that replaced lotteries and hearings with market-based Spectrum Auctions.
  • Commercializing the Net: The state of Michigan was the home of universities that created MERIT, which subsequently formed ANS to market internet access to commercial businesses.
  • IP Allocation: Regional Internet Registries (RIRs) are the bodies responsible for allocating IP addresses to ISPs.
  • IP Architecture: Legacy IPv4 is 32 bits, while the vastly expanded IPv6 is 128 bits.
